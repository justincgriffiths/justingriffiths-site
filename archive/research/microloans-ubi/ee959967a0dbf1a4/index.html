Who’s Afraid of Gen Z Socialism?

Jacobin · October 26, 2026

Relevance: 0.10

What’s on the mind of the ruling class these days? If the pages of The Economist are a reliable indicator, it’s the specter of “Gen Z socialism”: the rising support among young people for taxing billionaires, socializing the economy, and stopping the genocide in Gaza, as evidenced by the popularity of politicians like New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani, England’s Green Party leader Zack Polanski, or French presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon. Earlier this summer, the magazine dedicated a whole issue to “fighting back” against this “threat to prosperity,” calling on fellow free-market liberals to “stop apologizing” and level a “punchier defense of capitalism.” The rage bait worked: the magazine’s mailbox was flooded with critical responses, a selection of which it later published in a section titled “Were we too quick to attack Gen Z socialism?”The real pushback, however, has come from the streets. Around the world, Gen Z–led mass movements are decrying the rising cost of living and political corruption, as well as calling for better education, health care, and environmental protections. India is a case in point: on the very day that The Economist’s editorial was published, tens of thousands of angry young protesters flooded New Delhi, engaging in street battles with a disoriented and violent local police force and bringing the heart of the Indian capital to a standstill. The protests had been sparked by remarks made by Indian Chief Justice Surya Kant, who had compared unemployed young people to “cockroaches” and “parasites” during a Supreme Court hearing. Initially focused on educational accountability, the movement has since broadened its platform to include demands for electoral and economic reforms.India’s Cockroach protests are only the latest in a series of Gen Z uprisings that were initially largely concentrated in Asia and Africa. In Nepal, Madagascar, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, student movements toppled entire governments. Elsewhere protests were brutally repressed or even culminated in a bloody civil war, as in Myanmar.This fall has seen a spillover of youth-led mass mobilizations into various European countries. In France, Belgium, and Italy, high school students have been spearheading massive protests against educational austerity; in Spain and Portugal, young people are leading demonstrations against soaring housing costs; Albania’s “Flamingo Revolution” is calling for the resignation of the prime minister over ecologically and socially harmful tourism development, including a controversial resort backed by Jared Kushner, son-in-law of US President Donald Trump; and in Serbia a new Student List has just celebrated a historic victory, winning an absolute majority in Sunday’s parliamentary elections after two years of youth-led anti-corruption and anti-mining protests.But is this really all about generational difference?How Not to Think About GenerationsThe label “Gen Z” is hardly sufficient to describe such a diverse range of social movements. In many cases, it’s a cliché imposed from the outside by pundits with little understanding of local dynamics. At the same time, generation has undoubtedly become one of the key vectors along which movements organize and identify themselves, from Madagascar’s “Gen Z Mada” and Morocco’s “Gen Z 212” to Germany’s “Gen Z regelt.” “You messed with the wrong generation!” the rallying cry of Myanmar’s 2021 civil disobedience movement, has become a popular motto across the region. In France, the slogan “Génération 6–7 refait Mai 68&amp;quot; (Generation 6–7 is doing May ’68 all over again) has gone viral.How should socialists relate to such generation-based movements? Unlike class, race, or gender, generation is not a category that has traditionally been at the heart of socialist politics. Generational designations like “boomers,” “Gen X,” “millennials,” or “Gen Z” are not the product of Marxist theory but of predominantly US business consultants, pollsters, and think tanks like the Pew Research Center. Since the 1960s, US marketing corporations have built a profitable industry around studying the attitudes and preferences of supposed generational cohorts.It’s worth highlighting the sheer absurdity of the generational thinking that is at the heart of this corporate “science.” Its most influential articulation remains that of William Strauss and Neil Howe, two former congressional staffers, who mapped out their “theory” over a series of books, including Generations (1991) and The Fourth Turning (1997). Strauss and Howe argue that human history progresses in continuous eighty-to-one-hundred-year cycles. Each cycle corresponds to roughly one long human lifespan and can be broken down into four seasons (“turnings”), which always occur in the exact same sequence:First comes a high, a period of constant expansion. Next comes an awakening, a time of spiritual exploration and rebellion. Then comes an unraveling, in which individualism triumphs over crumbling institutions.
