Lula Must Win. Now It Looks Like He Can.

Jacobin · October 23, 2026

Relevance: 0.10

As Brazil heads to the polls on Sunday, the latest polls show Flávio Bolsonaro’s lead over incumbent president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva narrowing. One pollster, Qeast, had the eldest of Jair’s brood on 45 percent, just 1 point ahead of Lula.Flávio’s rejection rates have been steadily climbing since he flopped in last Sunday’s debate. While there is a good chance Brazil may still elect the crooked son of a convicted idiot, three weeks after his first-round victory, it’s also clear that Lula is back in the game thanks to a historic mobilization.Bolsonaro’s campaign has largely tried to keep him out of the limelight while portraying him as a more moderate version of his father and pushing anti-incumbent messaging through influencers and proxies. After winning the first round by 2 percent, Bolsonaro and his allies started acting as if the election was already in the bag and announcing bizarre potential cabinet appointments, such as a health minister opposed to the idea of health care as a universal right, which is enshrined in Brazil’s constitution. The right-winger’s pick (for now) as finance minister has talked about cutting the Bolsa Família welfare payment and other social programs.Flávio’s emergence from the shadows during the second round has been accompanied by a constant flood of scandals around him. One story that broke this week in Piaui magazine, Brazil’s equivalent of the New Yorker, encapsulated his brand of moderation. During the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, when a thousand of his countrymen were dying daily, he partied on a boat with a vaccine lobbyist trying to hawk overpriced doses. The lobbyist reportedly gifted him a Rolex and brought along some prostitutes for the ride. In essence, Flávio was pro-vaccination provided he got a cut of the profits, an orgy, and a watch out of it — unlike his more ideological brother Eduardo, who has embraced US-style anti-vax politics.After running a mostly complacent campaign for the first round this October 4, Lula’s campaign suddenly kicked into gear largely outside of the official structures of the Workers Party (PT). For instance, under the leadership of Minas Gerais lawmaker André Janones, there is a proper digital campaign this time around: a WhatsApp channel named “Lula’s Army” is deploying its now over 8.2 million followers on various missions. Targeted and organic online messaging, along with Flávio actually being forced to show the electorate who he really is, plus a historic wave of street mobilizations, give Lula a real chance of winning. Lula and the Left were also able to pass a historic victory for the labor movement in Brazil, reducing the working week to five days on, two days off on October 16.It remains to be seen if this will be enough to overcome US intervention, overt manipulation by Big Tech, dirty tricks, constant electoral intervention from the Supreme Electoral Court (TSE) judges, in particular President Kassio Nunes Marques, through rulings favorable to the Bolsonaro clan’s scion, and what has been exposed as a corrupt network in major media firms invested in ensuring Lula’s defeat. News website PL noted: “Bizarre rulings . . . including the censorship of a campaign spot that merely presented Flávio’s criminal ‘track record’ based on reports published by major media outlets.” Another Piauí story suggested that Justice Nunes Marques attended a party with sex workers and high-end meats organized by disgraced banker and “big brother” of Flávio, Daniel Vorcaro, who is best thought of as the tropical Jeffrey Epstein; one of his friends even joked on WhatsApp that Jeff was an amateur compared to Vorcaro.Post-Civil Society Electoral politicsThis is my third election in Brazil, and by far the most difficult to write about, in part because it has been, as Vincent Bevins puts it, “a post-civil society election.” Short-form video curated by individualized algorithms shapes political content in what remains of the public sphere more than traditional media or even the infamous disinformation channels on Telegram and WhatsApp used by the 2018 and 2022 Bolsonaro campaigns. Whereas last time there was a legion of YouTubers and budding podcasters shaping the narrative, this year there are thousands of Instagram and TikTok influencers doing that work; you would only know about them if you were locked into a particular algorithm from fitness to crypto and beauty for evangelical women.This was evident in the farcical TV debate last Sunday where a heavily coached and stimulated Flávio stalked Lula around the stage and adopted the sordid debate tactics of professional life coach and former São Paulo mayoral candidate Pablo Marçal, who is banned from politics until 2032 for lying about his assets in that campaign.
