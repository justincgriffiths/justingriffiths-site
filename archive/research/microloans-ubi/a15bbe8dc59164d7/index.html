Focus on the Real Drivers of the Affordability Crisis

Jacobin · October 27, 2026

Over the past five years, the price of consumer goods has dominated American political discourse. From Donald Trump to Zohran Mamdani, winning candidates have used “affordability politics” to articulate popular dissatisfaction with economic conditions. From the price of eggs to gas to burritos, the terms of discussion in the media, the campaign trail, and the proverbial kitchen table have revolved around the cost-of-living crisis embodied in these consumer goods.Although figures on the Right and center of the political scene have used inflation and affordability politics to bolster their agendas, during the second Trump administration, it is the Left and center left that have principally benefited from the salience of price talk. Indeed, the Democrats’ 2026 midterm messaging strategy has centered on blaming Trump’s Republicans for the cost-of-living crisis that Trump himself successfully pinned on Joe Biden just two years ago.That aggressive focus on the cost of living and a promise of consumer affordability will likely continue to be a successful playbook for both left and right anti-incumbent campaigns in the coming years. But in order for a political movement to actually address the cost-of-living crisis once in office, it needs a clearer picture of where those costs are coming from and how they have changed over time.Our affordability politics must go beyond lowering the price of eggs. And trends in American household spending underscore perhaps the central task of the Left: expanding the welfare state with universal, solidaristic, and visible social programs.Where Do Americans Spend Their Money?Anyone purchasing gas, groceries, cars, or airline tickets in the 2020s has experienced sticker shock since the onset of inflationary pressures catalyzed by COVID-19, tariffs, the artificial intelligence bubble, and the disastrous wars in Ukraine and Iran. But a longer view reveals a different picture.Compared to forty years ago, Americans today spend less of their total disposable income on food, transportation, clothing, and personal care. And they spend significantly more than they used to on health care, education, housing, and financial products (retirement contributions, nonmedical insurance, etc.). While we can see a modest rise in food and transportation budget shares from 2020–24, it does little to change the overall trends.When we look specifically at households with children, an even more dramatic story comes to the fore, in which the ballooning costs of education and childcare have nearly tripled their share of household spending.The roots of these contradictory dynamics lay in the neoliberal class compromise of the late 1970s, which resulted in lower prices for consumer goods but much higher prices for the assets and services that sustain social reproduction.After the labor and popular upsurges of the 1960s and ’70s were defeated, the weakened American working class accepted job cuts due to automation and offshoring, and the more vulnerable among domestic producers were forced to accept a new free trade regime that opened them up to global competition. While wages were lower, jobs more precarious, and employee benefits like pensions and health insurance increasingly scant, workers and their families could purchase food, clothing, electronics, and consumer durables (appliances, furniture, automobiles, etc.) at lower prices.The shift of world garment production to the Global South during the globalization waves of the 1980s–2000s has driven down US household budget allocations for apparel by a whopping 60 percent. The declines are more modest but still meaningful (roughly 15 percent) in food and transportation. Again, these price changes are linked to the globalization of supply chains in the food, automobile, and energy sectors.As this process unfolded, American workers and households were also increasingly forced to bear the monetary costs of their own social reproduction. Postindustrial developments in the US economy reinforced changing cultural mores to spur shifts in family and education. The end of the “breadwinner wage” and greater gender freedoms meant more women in the paid workforce, fewer households with parents acting as full-time caregivers, and greater numbers of people funneled into higher education to prepare for the medical, education, and knowledge economy careers that required such credentials.Millions more Americans were paying for education, from daycare to graduate school. And the costs of that education kept rising.Over this same period, the US health care system experienced a weakening of employer-based health coverage for unionized workers, and increasingly powerful and consolidated insurance, medical device, hospital, and pharmaceutical corporations. These dynamics, along with longer lifespans, have driven astronomical rises in costs across the board.
