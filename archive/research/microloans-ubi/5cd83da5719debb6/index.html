There’s No End in Sight to Yemen’s Destructive War

Jacobin · October 23, 2026

hezbollah
Relevance: 0.33

Yet again, the world is failing to give Yemen the attention it deserves. Well over a month into the renewal of the war between Ansar Allah, known as the Huthis, and the internationally recognized government (IRG), there have already been thousands of military and civilian casualties on both sides.Fierce fighting is going on in different parts of the country, and more than 250,000 people have been displaced, most of whom are moving from one active front line to areas that might be swept up in the conflict within hours.The humanitarian situation is disastrous, with famine threatening in some parts of the country. One of Yemen’s largest cities, Taiz, is under siege by the Huthis, with hardly any medical supplies, food, or water arriving for the population, now swollen by the 120,000 or so people displaced within the governorate.All of this deserves to be the focus of international concern. The return of full-scale fighting in Yemen was expected. Throughout the country, few favored continuation of the “no war, no peace” situation that had prevailed since the 2022 temporary truce.Despite this, the main events to have grabbed the headlines are the few Huthi drones and missiles that successfully attacked airports and some oil facilities in Riyadh and other locations in Saudi Arabia. Admittedly, these attacks have killed close to twenty people and wounded more than three hundred, and they are an important political development for the kingdom.They also demonstrate that the regional conflict has expanded way beyond both Gaza and Iran, thanks to Donald Trump’s recklessness in going to war in support of Benjamin Netanyahu’s projects. While there is a clear link between the Iran war and what is going on in Yemen and Saudi Arabia, the issues in the two centers of conflict are distinct, and each could exist without the other. But are lives lost in Riyadh worth more than those in Taiz or Sana’a?Back to WarSince July 2026, the war in Yemen has resumed at a fiercer and more destructive tempo than its original outbreak in 2015. Fighting on the ground is costing hundreds of lives, mainly among the Huthi troops whose leaders are sending waves of young and inexperienced fighters, many of whom were forcibly recruited, to the front lines in pursuit of their offensive.Fighting continues in the northern areas of Marib and Sa’ada bordering Saudi Arabia. In early September, Ansar Allah took control of the southern part of the Yemeni coast, including both the Bab al-Mandab Strait and Mokha. It achieved this rapid victory thanks to three main factors: its own command efficiency, continued division and lack of coordination among its opponents, and the use of advanced technology, including artificial intelligence misinformation systems.The period since the 2022 truce allowed the Huthis to strengthen and improve their strategy and acquire even more sophisticated weaponry. Their intervention against Israel between late 2023 and 2025 gave them an international popularity boost. Links and support from Hezbollah and Iran contributed to their military skills, assisted by the purchase of advanced technological elements on the international market.They have also benefited from AI advances, as was demonstrated recently by their attempted use of Anthropic’s Claude to develop a missile and then to identify the cause of its failure. The Huthis have long since demonstrated the absurdity of dismissing their movement as a bunch of wild, sandal-wearing tribesmen armed with handguns.Partly as a consequence of the military battering suffered in 2025 from the fifty-two-day US bombing campaign and a number of well-targeted Israeli air strikes, Ansar Allah initially chose to stay on the sidelines of the US-Israeli war against Iran. Instead of closing the Bab al-Mandab Strait altogether, following Iranian statements that this option was an additional weapon in the struggle to control the world hydrocarbon market, the Huthis limited their blockade to Saudi shipping.
