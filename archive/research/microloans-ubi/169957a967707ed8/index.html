When Gas Diplomacy Sounds Like Regime Change

Jacobin · October 25, 2026

Relevance: 0.10

“We can do this to any country we want. We can do it here too.” According to a Wall Street Journal investigation published on September 25, Kimberly Guilfoyle, Donald Trump’s ambassador to Greece, conveyed this message during a March dinner in Athens while discussing the expected fall of the Romanian government with Greek Minister for the Environment and Energy Stavros Papastavrou. Papastavrou replied that the United States could not replace a Greek government elected by the Greek people.Although Guilfoyle’s lawyer disputes the newspaper’s description of the exchange, the report immediately caused political controversy in both Greece and Romania. In Greece, the alleged words landed on particularly sensitive ground. They sounded less like an eccentric diplomatic indiscretion than an updated, Trumpian version of a much older doctrine through which Washington decides how much sovereignty smaller countries are allowed to exercise.The story has since taken another turn. On October 13, the White House asked Guilfoyle to resign amid a widening series of misconduct allegations, all of which she denies. These went well beyond the reported conversation about governments in Greece and Romania and included allegations involving gifts and favors, the use of official access, contacts with Greek business interests and lobbyists, and her conduct in office.Yet the allegations surrounding Guilfoyle’s departure are only one part of the story. Her own account of what she believed she had accomplished in Greece points to the broader political and economic project behind her tenure. Her farewell statement deserves credit for its candor about the political economy of her mission.Guilfoyle said she had worked to deliver “meaningful victories for the American people.” She pointed to expanded US liquefied natural gas (LNG) exports through the Vertical Corridor, new opportunities for American businesses and investment, stronger “American energy dominance,” and a United States that remains the “indispensable leader in Southeast Europe.” The beneficiaries she chose to name were not Greek households struggling with energy bills but the American people, American businesses, and American geopolitical power.The latest scandal was not Guilfoyle’s first collision with Greek public opinion. Her relationship with the country had started badly before she even took up the post. During the 2015 debt crisis, after Greek voters rejected a new austerity package, Guilfoyle called Greeks “freeloaders” on Fox News and told them to “suck it up.” She also complained that they retired too early and compared the need to discipline them to training a dog that urinates on a rug.The comments resurfaced when Trump nominated her for the Athens post and provoked sharp criticism in Greece. Guilfoyle subsequently stressed her “love and admiration” for Greece in public but issued no retraction or apology for the original remarks.The Business Behind the ScandalThe Wall Street Journal investigation is fundamentally a story about energy, business, and lobbying. At its center is Christos Marafatsos, a registered lobbyist for AKTOR Group, one of Greece’s largest construction and infrastructure companies.According to the Wall Street Journal and federal lobbying records cited by the House Foreign Affairs Committee, AKTOR paid Marafatsos $160,000 through June 2026 to establish contacts with the Department of State, Department of Energy, and the Executive Office of the President. He accompanied Guilfoyle to official meetings, where some participants reportedly assumed that he was part of her staff.Guilfoyle has simultaneously been one of the most visible advocates of expanding American LNG exports through Greece and across Southeast Europe. AKTOR has positioned itself to benefit heavily from that expansion.Through Atlantic SEE LNG Trade, a joint venture with the state-owned Public Gas Corporation of Greece (DEPA), the company has signed long-term deals around the import and transport of US LNG through the Vertical Corridor. Beginning in 2030, the venture is contracted to buy at least one million metric tons of American LNG every year for twenty years under its expanded agreement with Venture Global.AKTOR’s own projections demonstrate the scale of the opportunity. The company says LNG agreements already signed or in development could eventually generate around €90 million in annual EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization) from 2030, or around €1.8 billion over twenty years. These are company projections, not guaranteed profits. But they make clear why the emerging gas route is not simply a matter of abstract geopolitics.The Vertical Gas Corridor is supposed to move gas from Greek LNG terminals north through Bulgaria and Romania toward Moldova, Ukraine, and Central Europe.
