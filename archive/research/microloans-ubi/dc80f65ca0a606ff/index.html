Financial Capital Didn’t Make New York City Great

Jacobin · October 27, 2026

It’s the beginning of the new Ric Burns documentary on New York City, and Adam Gopnik is choking up.He’s talking about the diversity and tolerance that New York, at its best, embodies. He’s talking about the challenges the city has faced over the past fifty years. Above all, he’s talking about how it met those challenges: through its willingness to adapt. Cities are “self-emergent” and “self-sustaining” forms, Gopnik argues, but self-sustaining requires transformation.Happily, New York has been up to the task. “We accept [New York’s] remaking of itself again and again,” he avers, and it is this tradition of demographic and developmental pluralism (“plural pluralisms,” in his inimitable phrasing) that allowed Gotham to transcend its postwar decay and become the beacon of cosmopolitan, progressive liberalism we adore today.It’s a powerful way to open. It’s a message that many other bards of Gotham, from Walt Whitman and Jane Jacobs to Richard Sennett and Teju Cole, have proclaimed. But it’s a message almost entirely undercut by the structure and argument of the documentary that follows.That’s because, in the two latest episodes of Ric Burns’s long-running series New York — whose first five episodes aired in 1999, with two following in 2001 and another installment in 2003 — postwar Gotham is not saved by its “self-emergent” forms or the bottom-up moxie of its existing population but instead by the top-down, outside-in forces of globalization and capitalist development. In 1961, Jane Jacobs wrote that “cities have the capability of providing something for everybody, only because, and only when, they are created by everybody.” The New York City shown here is, for the most part, created by everybody except the ordinary people who live there.To be fair, in an interview about the documentary for the Hollywood Reporter, Burns called “capitalism and democracy and globalization” the “three great pieces of uranium in the city’s engine.” But democracy plays a minor role in this tale. We do not see, as in the series’ original episodes, how unions and tenant organizers struggled to bring Gotham’s economy under greater popular control. Nor do we hear from left-wing scholars like the late Marshall Berman and Mike Wallace who, in previous installments, added a grain of skepticism to the self-congratulating narratives spun by the city’s power brokers. Instead, we hear the story of Gotham’s redemption since the 1970s as the long, lonely struggle to make the city safe for globalized capitalism — accompanied, of course, by sweeping pans and saccharine Irish reels.This is not to say that ordinary New Yorkers are absent from this story. To the contrary: the documentary admirably recounts the patterns of cultural and social transformations that have coursed through the city over the past fifty years. But such pluralism, and the city’s broader liberal political culture, is framed here as dependent on the city’s successful courting of globalized capital since the 1970s — whereas the truth is that globalized capital in its current form now threatens those very virtues that New Yorkers hold dear.Fall and Rise?Every redemption arc requires a fall, and the first episode locates New York City’s fall in its postwar deindustrialization. We hear in admirably clear terms how the loss of manufacturing threatened both the city’s fiscal health and its heavily unionized, heavily working-class population. But the film blames deindustrialization on the impersonal, irresistible forces of “globalization” to which Gotham could either adapt or, in the narrator’s portentous words, “plummet into the widening abyss opening up between the past and the future.”This rhetoric, with its Clintonesque intonations of globalized inevitability, distorts the past. Historian Kenneth Jackson claims in the film that “the city tried to preserve those industrial districts.” Yet Joshua Freeman, a better-informed source on this point, writes in Working-Class New York that the city’s postwar leaders were “sanguine” about the flight of industrial jobs. Well before the mayoralty of Michael Bloomberg and his whiz kids, city leaders and bodies like the Regional Plan Association believed that New York needed to displace manufacturing in favor of globally oriented information and financial service industries.The series itself once said as much: in its earlier episode on the World Trade Center, historian Mike Wallace narrated how “monopoly capitalists” (his words) used public subsidies, zoning reforms, and other tools to “expand the financial core” of downtown at the expense of working-class manufacturing jobs. This process of financialization did not follow the loss of manufacturing; financialization accompanied and accelerated that loss.Why does this matter? The documentary attributes the city’s fiscal crisis of the 1970s to the “inevitable” loss of the city’s manufacturing tax base.
