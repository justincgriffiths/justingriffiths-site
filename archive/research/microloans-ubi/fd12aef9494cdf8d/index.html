Vancouver Is in the Middle of a Socialist Surge

Jacobin · October 23, 2026

Relevance: 0.10

Vancouver’s contrasts are visible on its streets. Luxury cars cruise past some of North America’s worst homeless encampments. Last Saturday, October 17, the city produced a political contrast to match: after four years of a center-right city hall, its municipal council has transformed into one of the most left-wing in North America.The breakthrough comes alongside strong socialist showings in municipal elections around the world, from Zohran Mamdani’s win in New York to Die Linke’s recent state election victory in Berlin. The Coalition of Progressive Electors (COPE), a democratic socialist party, elected all twelve candidates it ran: four to council, four to school board, and four to park board. COPE’s Sean Orr topped the council ballot in raw votes. Together with the four Green councilors, OneCity’s sole council seat, and Green Mayor Pete Fry, COPE’s four council wins give the progressive bloc nine of ten councilor seats and the mayor’s office — ten of city hall’s eleven elected seats.As right-leaning candidates made gains in other British Columbia municipalities, and as the social democratic New Democratic Party fought to retain power in the October 24 provincial election, COPE’s result testified to the party’s hard work and organizing efforts. It vindicated a party that was presumed dead after its near wipeout four years ago. The Breach reported in depth on COPE’s lengthy effort to rebuild its membership and organizing capacity, give members a meaningful role in shaping the party, and embrace its socialist identity rather than blur it into generic progressivism. That work positioned COPE to capitalize on the growing working-class discontent and the material realities of everyday life in Vancouver.A City in CrisisVancouver’s housing crisis formed the backdrop to COPE’s victory. More than half of the city’s households rent. In 2021, approximately 39 percent of renter households spent at least 30 percent of their income on shelter, while roughly one-quarter were in core housing need: their homes were unaffordable, unsuitable, or inadequate, and they could not afford acceptable alternatives. The problem extends beyond the poorest residents. Even relatively well-paid workers can struggle to find housing near their jobs, while homeownership is increasingly out of reach for younger people. Economic insecurity has become a defining feature of everyday life in the city.ABC Vancouver came to power promising to address the problem by speeding up approvals and building more housing. Its administration can point to real increases in housing approvals and rental development. But more construction hasn’t made Vancouver affordable for many of the people who need homes.In September 2026, a report found that more than five thousand completed condominiums across Metro Vancouver remained unsold, with an estimated value of $4.45 billion. The chasm between new housing and what people can afford was stark. Mayor Ken Sim’s city council also shuttered the Vancouver Renters Office, which provided resources and information on tenant rights and how to navigate the legal system.Homelessness also increased in Vancouver under ABC. In contrast, COPE’s platform called for the city to acquire existing apartment buildings and convert them into permanently affordable public housing and co-ops. It also proposed a tax on properties worth more than $5 million to fund efforts to end homelessness, tripling the Empty Homes Tax, as well as stronger tenant protections. It put housing and care at the center of its strategy to respond to the city’s interlocking problems.That emphasis was particularly significant in the Downtown Eastside, where COPE has historically maintained a strong base. The neighborhood has been at the center of Vancouver’s overlapping problems of poverty, housing insecurity, and drug poisoning. Its residents have endured these conditions through successive governments that have promised to restore order without providing the necessary housing and care.The CampaignOn Election Day, volunteers reportedly knocked on more than ten thousand doors. The campaign gave voters a positive vision of the city, championing socialized housing, public grocers, and expanded public services like Wi-Fi, libraries, and washrooms. Its “Fight for Vancouver” platform set out a high-level road map.The slogan, “Evict Ken Sim,” aimed at the outgoing right-wing mayor, became a meme, appearing in art, on buttons dotting the city, and on campaign merch like T-shirts. COPE’s younger candidates, including Solomon Yi-Kieran, Vancouver’s youngest-ever city councilor, and Chloe Leslie, brought passion and vitality to the party’s communications. Rather than imitating Zohran Mamdani’s style, they drew on Vancouver’s own culture, making high-energy, often playful social media videos.The new council has an opportunity to reverse some of ABC’s policies and pursue shared progressive priorities. But a progressive majority is not the same thing as a socialist government
