Milwaukee’s Proud History of Baseball — and Socialism

Jacobin · October 23, 2026

Relevance: 0.10

Organizers like to say that all politics boils down to a battle between organized money and organized people. Candidates with the largest war chests tend to win, but contenders who refuse to take donations from corporate interests can prevail if their supporters are well-organized, inspired, and strategic. Likewise, big employers can spend huge sums on union busting, but employees can win union organizing drives if they know how to mobilize workers to stick together and not be intimidated.The same is true in baseball. Teams in big markets with large payrolls have a decided advantage when it comes to recruiting talented players who command huge salaries, but feisty, scrappy teams with fewer stars can sometimes win championships. Since 2000, more than 80 percent of the teams that have advanced to the World Series ranked in the top half of league payrolls. Teams with the five largest payrolls (out of thirty teams) account for roughly 38 percent of all World Series contestants and nearly half (48 percent) of all World Series champions.But this year is an exception: the World Series contestants — the Milwaukee Brewers and the Tampa Bay Rays — are both among the poorer teams in baseball. Milwaukee is Major League Baseball’s smallest market in terms of population size and TV audience and ranks nineteenth in payroll ($143.5 million). Tampa Bay is twenty-fifth in market size and ranks twenty-sixth in payroll ($98.3 million).The top-spending teams are the New York Mets ($346.8 million) and the Los Angeles Dodgers ($338 million). Despite the disparity, the Brewers swept the Dodgers in four games in the National League Championship Series to make it to the World Series, thwarting LA’s hopes to win three consecutive World Series. The Brewers and Rays won their respective leagues by combining strong pitching, agile and savvy “small ball” play, and because of managers who utilized their rosters to get the most out of teams without many highly paid superstars.During the first half of the twentieth century, in both politics and baseball, Milwaukee’s have-nots often beat the odds. And indeed, those two histories are intertwined in ways not often appreciated.Milwaukee’s Sewer SocialistsIn the early 1900s, Milwaukee’s socialists came to power by forging an alliance with the city’s labor movement. The city’s culture was heavily shaped by German immigrants, whose athletic and gymnastic clubs embedded the ideal of a “sound mind in a sound body.” One of them, Turner Hall, built in 1883 near the city’s downtown, served as a gathering place for Milwaukee’s radicals and socialists. It provided its members a place to socialize, listen to music, drink beer, and discuss politics. Eugene Debs gave his first speech as a Socialist there in 1898 and spoke at Turner Hall on many other occasions. (It remains a popular site for meetings and performances, with a gym in the basement.)One of Turner Hall’s members was Victor Berger, an Austrian Jewish immigrant who spearheaded the Socialist Party’s rise to local power. Berger was a schoolteacher, an editor of two newspapers (one in German, another in English), and a skilled organizer. He and Debs helped found the Socialist Party of America (SP) in 1901. Berger built the Milwaukee branch into a formidable political machine. He secured the backing of the unions, including the powerful brewery workers’ locals and most German and Polish immigrants. The SP sponsored carnivals, picnics, singing societies, and even Sunday schools, conducting their campaigns in English, German, and Polish.In 1910, Milwaukee voters sent Berger to Washington as the country’s first socialist congressman. He lost his seat in 1912 but was elected again in 1918 and in a 1919 special election; both times the House refused to seat him because of his opposition to World War I. He returned to Congress in 1922, served three terms, and lost his seat in 1928.Berger sponsored bills for old-age pensions, government ownership of the radio industry, abolition of child labor, self-government for the District of Columbia, and a system of public works to provide relief for the unemployed. He also sponsored resolutions to withdraw federal troops from the Mexican border, for the abolition of the Senate (which was then not yet elected directly by the voters and was nicknamed the “millionaires’ club”), for women’s suffrage, and for federal ownership of the railroads.While none of Berger’s proposals gained much traction in Congress, his comrades in Milwaukee were a dominant force in the city’s civic life. In 1912, some 1,200 SP members held public office in 340 cities and towns across the United States, including Socialist mayors in Schenectady and Berkeley; Socialists would later win the mayor’s office in Minneapolis (1916) and Reading (1927). But Socialists’ most enduring success emerged in Milwaukee.
