We Can Beat the Right With a Left-Wing Affordability Agenda

Jacobin · October 22, 2026

inequality
Relevance: 0.33

In the past decade, much ink has been spilled over the question of whether we are witnessing a resurgence of fascism. Those debates have calmed since last November, when Donald Trump casually identified himself as a fascist amid a disorientingly effusive meeting with New York City’s Mayor-elect Zohran Mamdani.Part of the problem with the debate is that fascism can be difficult to define and identify — a point economist Isabella Weber makes at the outset of her new book, Anti-Fascist Economics. Unlike other major political ideologies, fascism has few defining texts, social theories, or economic platforms. However, she adds, there are “unifying themes — and an obsession with restoring the dominance of the chosen group by authoritarian and violent means is key.”To my mind, fascist ideology is captured well enough by its original symbol, the fasces: a hatchet made of tightly bound rods and an axe head, representing the fierce unity of the nation on the one hand and its outward-facing aggression on the other. As Matt Christman once described it, “The cultural expression of fascism is a Janus face of performative sentimentality and performative cruelty.” To take the point a little further, fascism’s programmatic vagueness means that we may in fact be on better footing if we regard it as a cultural phenomenon with political aspirations. That is more or less the approach offered by Weber and by Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor in their equally insightful new book, End Times Fascism.Both books make a persuasive case that the contemporary swell of revanchism has resulted from rising levels of economic stress and valid anxieties across vast swathes of Global North society. When a sense of scarcity takes hold, the Right can make a compelling argument for the abandonment of solidaristic values in favor of bunkerism. That we are confronting this crisis in a moment of unprecedented levels of automation and global integration points to a profound failure of the neoliberal doctrines that have shaped the global system.Weber’s work offers one of the most systematic critiques of those doctrines, as well as a plausible set of alternative policies. At the heart of her argument is a basic refutation of the neoliberal position that there is no viable middle ground between a totalitarian command economy and the free market. Indeed, as she notes repeatedly, neoliberals have consistently shown a preference for free-market authoritarianism over social democracy. Today, she argues, we must recognize that the latter is more viable than mainstream thought has led us to believe.The intellectual leaders of neoliberalism — notably Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman — are best known for their attacks on the modern welfare state. But an equally central neoliberal dogma is that price controls are necessarily doomed to fail in private markets, so that governments must choose between comprehensive price-setting or complete laissez-faire. The standard price mechanism suggests that if prices are set too low, there will be a shortage of goods; too high, and there will be a surfeit of unsold product. One of the essential premises of modern economic thought is that the ideal market-clearing price can only be determined in fully private markets.This second argument has conquered Northern minds almost completely for the past half-century, making it one of the most sacred cows in modern economic thought. Weber reveals that this faith is unwarranted. Simply put, it does not follow from the standard price mechanism that commodity markets should be fully private or unregulated.The problem is not merely that private markets fall prey to monopolistic power; it is that they are prone to crises that exacerbate poverty and inequality. Supply shocks can emerge from environmental disasters, wars, and pandemics — and when this occurs, prices will spike in the affected industries, with potentially dangerous political effects. As Weber puts it, “Commodity markets are inherently volatile, and the human cost of leaving them entirely to private participants falls hardest on those least able to absorb it, while carrying the potential to destabilize whole economies and societies.”This argument, drawing from neglected histories of successful price control policies, amounts to a significant departure from the prevailing wisdom. The option of price controls is especially vital because, she argues, mainstream economists have gravely mischaracterized the drivers of inflation: far from an excess of demand, the main problem is a form of “sellers’ inflation,” which occurs when price shocks facilitate corporate price gouging at the expense of the general public.Her work thus points in a clear direction for anyone seeking practical strategies for economic stabilization in an era of overlapping crises.
