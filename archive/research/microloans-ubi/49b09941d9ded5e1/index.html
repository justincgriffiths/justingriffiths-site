1956 Was a Missed Opportunity for Socialist Reform in Poland

Jacobin · October 26, 2026

Relevance: 0.10

In June 1956, a delegation of renowned British left-wing economists, including Joan Robinson and Maurice Dobb, traveled to Poland to learn about its socialist transformation. The newly installed socialist government had driven through a program of economic modernization, as the country industrialized and urbanized, and free universal health care and education were being provided for the first time.While visiting the city of Poznań, they expected to admire the material achievements of the new Polish People’s Republic (PRL). Instead they witnessed Polish soldiers manning Soviet tanks as they put down a workers’ revolt. Living standards had not kept up with industrial expansion, and a wave of strikes and demonstrations broke out.Government repression left an estimated fifty-seven people dead — the largest single such action in the PRL’s history. The delegation left with many of its illusions shattered, as Poland sank deeper into an existential political crisis.The Opening of ReformA period of liberalization had preceded the events of 1956. Joseph Stalin’s death in 1953 enlarged the space for political and economic reform in Eastern Europe, and the end of the Korean War the same year eased Cold War tensions. Room opened for alternative socialist economic paths: the USSR developed friendlier relations with Yugoslavia, whose leaders had broken with Stalin in 1948, and Hungary halted collectivization.Pressure for reform built within the ruling Polish United Workers’ Party (PZPR), as reformers sought greater economic and political decentralization. Critical voices grew louder in the press, especially about economic inefficiencies, bureaucratic mismanagement, wasted resources, and stagnating living standards.A current of alternative Marxist economic thinking took center stage. Grouped around two world-renowned economists, Michał Kalecki and Oskar Lange, it developed somewhat competing reform programs.Poland faced problems common to all twentieth-century transitions to socialism. Building socialism in a poor, semifeudal country devastated by Nazi occupation and war proved no easy task. Initial heavy industrialization brought impressive results (rapid urbanization, economic growth, and the expansion of welfare), yet the overemphasis on heavy industry and neglect of services and consumption fueled working-class discontent.Kalecki proposed rebalancing the economy through prioritizing investment in sectors that would directly improve the living standards of the population. Lange (and his collaborators, including Włodzimierz Brus and Tadeusz Kowalik) drew inspiration from Yugoslavia’s decentralized model of workers’ councils and workplace self-management.Behind these debates lay a growing realization that socialism could not be created rapidly through government decrees. It was a long historical process in which features of the past (markets, money, private property) would persist for some time.Workers Rise, Leaders ShiftBy the mid-1950s, pressure from below had spread across the socialist bloc, with strikes and demonstrations in Czechoslovakia, East Germany, and, most notably, Hungary. The June events in Poznań sparked a wave of protests throughout Poland, as workers demanded higher wages and better conditions. Workers’ councils sprang up across the country, as employees sought participation in management, the decentralization of decision-making, and a reduction of the bureaucracy’s power.These protests unfolded against a backdrop of change at the top. In December 1954, Władysław Gomułka was freed after spending four years in prison. Gomułka had led postwar Poland until 1948, when Stalin accused him of nationalist deviations. After his release, he advocated an alternative “Polish road to socialism,” distinct from the Soviet model.Reformist pressure intensified after the PZPR took the unique step in the Eastern Bloc of circulating Nikita Khrushchev’s “secret speech,” which had been delivered at the Twentieth Congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union on February 25, 1956, among its party members. Gomułka was reinstated to the Politburo, becoming the unifying figure for opposition currents within the party.However, his call for a distinct form of Polish socialism and demands to remove Soviet officers and advisers from the Polish armed forces, security apparatus, and Politburo brought him into direct conflict with Moscow.The October ShowdownOn October 19, 1956, the day the Eighth Plenum of the Central Committee of the Polish United Workers’ Party was due to start, a Soviet delegation, including Khrushchev, landed unannounced in Warsaw, seeking to halt Gomułka’s reforms. Talks ensued as news spread that Soviet troops were heading toward the Polish border. Sections of the Polish army and groups of workers prepared to defend the capital.
