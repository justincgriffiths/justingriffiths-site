US/Iran conflict: nuclear negotiations, sanctions regime, and the 2025-2026 escalation

US/Iran conflict: nuclear negotiations, sanctions regime, and the 2025-2026 escalation

April 25, 2026·2 min read·7.8Converged

Core Arguments

  1. SANCTIONS REGIME: The sanctions regime has not been static since 2015. For instance, in June 2018, the Trump administration reimposed sanctions after the JCPOA was terminated. This period is often referred to as "maximum pressure," which lasted from June 2018 to January 2021 (source: Carnegie Endowment for International Peace); this clearly indicates that the sanctions regime has undergone significant changes and is not in place since just 2015.

  2. NUCLEAR NEGOTIATIONS: The focus on disarmament was not static or exclusive. After the US withdrew from the JCPOA in May 2018, Iran responded by increasing uranium enrichment levels (source: Carnegie Endowment for International Peace). Additionally, Iran engaged in non-nuclear-related negotiations on issues such as human rights and regional conflicts (e.g., Yemen). This diversification of the negotiation agenda suggests that the initial focus on disarmament was not static or exclusive.

  3. ESCALATION: The timeframe of escalation between 2025-2026 is questionable given historical context. While there were instances of heightened tensions, including the assassination of Iranian General Soleimani in January 2020 by a US drone strike and Iran's subsequent missile strikes on Saudi Arabia, these events did not constitute an escalation spanning from just those years (source: The Diplomat).

Counterargument Summary

My counterargument provides a more nuanced understanding of the sanctions regime, nuclear negotiations, and the timeframe of escalations. It highlights that while there were significant changes in the sanctions regime and diversification in the negotiation agenda, these factors are not sufficient to discredit the original research's basic claims about the conflict’s dynamics.
