# US/Iran Conflict Intelligence Briefing **July 13, 2026**

The Strait of Hormuz has moved from a theoretical flashpoint to an active military theater, with both the United States and Iran conducting operations that risk triggering an uncontrolled escalation spiral at the world's most critical energy chokepoint. Simultaneously, Iran's proxy networks are demonstrating unprecedented geographic reach, striking targets in the UK while maintaining pressure across the Middle East—a dual-front strategy that leaves the international community without effective diplomatic countermeasures.

**The Strait of Hormuz is now a live conflict zone.** According to Geopolitics Unplugged's cross-referenced reporting from CENTCOM statements, Reuters, and Iranian state media, verified military operations occurred on July 12-13 with both sides claiming closure or near-closure of the waterway. The specifics matter: roughly 21 percent of global petroleum passes through the Strait daily, making even temporary disruptions economically catastrophic. What distinguishes this moment from previous saber-rattling is the shift from rhetorical threats to kinetic activity. CENTCOM has confirmed US strike operations, while Iranian state media claims Tehran has closed or severely restricted traffic. The gap between these claims—where one side's "temporary closure" is another's "blockade"—creates dangerous ambiguity about whether either party fully understands the other's red lines. Energy markets are already pricing in supply disruption risk, but the real danger lies in the feedback loop: each side interprets the other's military moves as confirmation of hostile intent, justifying further escalation.

**Iran's proxy apparatus has achieved a troubling geographic expansion that complicates Western response options.** The UK's designation of the Islamic Movement of the Companions of the Right (IMCR) as an Iran-linked group responsible for antisemitic arson attacks on British Jewish sites represents a qualitative shift in how Tehran's networks operate. This is no longer confined to Hezbollah in Lebanon, Houthi operations in the Red Sea, or militia activity in Iraq and Syria. By orchestrating attacks in the UK—a NATO ally with direct intelligence relationships to the US—Iran has demonstrated it can create pressure across multiple theaters simultaneously. Fox News World reports that British officials explicitly blamed Tehran for the IMCR campaign, prompting the government to ban the IRGC outright and expand powers to counter foreign sabotage. The strategic logic is clear: while US and regional attention focuses on the Strait and Gaza-adjacent conflicts, Iran-backed actors can impose costs on Western allies far from traditional Middle Eastern battlegrounds. This distributes Western defensive resources and signals to Tehran's regional allies that Iran can strike beyond the immediate conflict zone.

**The diplomatic vacuum is the most dangerous element.** James M. Dorsey's assessment in Turbulent World captures the paralysis: the international community is "holding its breath" rather than actively de-escalating. Oman, traditionally a backchannel for US-Iran communication, has not publicly brokered any negotiations in recent days. The UK's IRGC ban, while symbolically important, is a response to escalation rather than a tool for managing it. Neither the US nor Iran appears to have activated diplomatic off-ramps. This matters because military incidents in confined spaces like the Strait can escalate faster than decision-makers can respond. A miscalculation—a US vessel interpreting Iranian naval movements as hostile, or an Iranian commander firing on what he believes is a provocation—could trigger a cycle neither side intended.

**Watch next:** Monitor whether Oman re-engages as a mediator in the coming 48-72 hours, track CENTCOM statements for indicators of whether the US views Hormuz operations as temporary or sustained, and assess whether the UK's IRGC ban triggers similar designations from the EU or Canada that could signal coordinated Western pressure or, conversely, further isolate diplomatic channels.
