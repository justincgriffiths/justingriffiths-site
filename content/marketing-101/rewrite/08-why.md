## Where it comes from

Simon Sinek drew the Golden Circle in a September 2009 TEDx talk, ["How great leaders inspire action"](https://www.ted.com/talks/simon_sinek_how_great_leaders_inspire_action). It had more than 71 million plays on TED.com as of November 2026. He also drew it in his book *Start with Why* (Portfolio, 2009).

His line from the talk: "People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it." His three examples are Apple, Martin Luther King Jr. and the Wright brothers.

The claim you are testing is that the order matters. Most brands say what they make, then how. The ones people follow start with what they believe. The product comes last.

## Apple: "1984" and "Think Different"

&#91;embedded content: Apple · why → how → what, 2 campaigns\]

You are looking at two campaigns, 13 years apart, with the same why. "1984" launched the Macintosh and barely showed it. The Macintosh went on sale two days later at $2,495.

"Think Different" (TBWA\Chiat\Day) arrived when Apple was losing money after Steve Jobs came back. Jobs explained it in 1997: "To me, marketing is about values." He used Nike as his model: "Nike sells the commodity. They sell shoes," but its ads honor great athletes.

Read the bottom line of each card as timing, not proof. Sales and profit also depended on the products and their prices.

## Nike and Colin Kaepernick: Dream Crazy (2018)

Nike deliberately made a polarizing choice by choosing former NFL quarterback Colin Kaepernick. CBS Sports called him "the face of the 30-year anniversary of the 'Just Do It' campaign".

The ad's tagline: "Don't ask if your dreams are crazy. Ask if they're crazy enough." Its most-quoted line: "Believe in something, even if it means sacrificing everything."

## Words to know

| Term | Plain meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand | What you believe about a company, not its logo | "Apple is for creative people" |
| Purpose (the why) | The belief a company stands for, beyond making money | Nike: "If you have a body, you are an athlete" |
| Positioning | The spot a brand holds in your head, compared with rivals | Mac vs. PC |
| Brand equity | The extra you will pay, or the loyalty you show, for the name alone | You pay more for the swoosh than for a plain shoe |
| Polarizing campaign | A campaign that knowingly divides its audience to strengthen its bond with part of it | Dream Crazy |
| Earned media value | A dollar estimate of free coverage a campaign got. It is an estimate, not revenue. | "$X of free press in a day" |

## Why it matters in marketing

A product changes every year; a belief does not. The why gives you one message to repeat across campaigns for decades. It also helps you and your team decide which ideas to say no to.

## How it connects to business

You are back in session 1. A strong why shifts the demand curve out and makes it steeper: fans pay more and switch less. It is also the hardest effect for you to count (session 6). A revenue chart that rises after a campaign shows you timing, not cause.

## Check yourself

1. Write Nike's why in one sentence without the word "shoes."
2. Nike's revenue grew in the quarter after Dream Crazy. What would you need to show the ad *caused* it?

**Answers:** 1. You can name any belief, not a product. Nike's careers page leads with "If you have a body, you are an athlete" ([Nike Careers](https://careers.nike.com/)). 2. You need a comparison: matched places or people who did not see the ad. That is the incrementality test from session 2.
